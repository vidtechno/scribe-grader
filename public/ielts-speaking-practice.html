IELTS Speaking guide

IELTS Speaking practice: record, listen and improve across all three parts

The test rewards clear, connected speech more than memorised lines. Use these original questions to practise answering naturally, then listen for the specific habits you can change.

Know the format

The official IELTS Speaking test is a recorded, face-to-face interview lasting about 11–14 minutes. Part 1 covers familiar topics; Part 2 is a longer turn based on a cue card with one minute to prepare; Part 3 develops the topic into a more general discussion.

IELTS assesses fluency and coherence, lexical resource, grammatical range and accuracy, and pronunciation. A practice recording helps you notice these features, although an AI estimate is not an official band score.

Part 1: give a direct answer and one useful detail

Try these original practice questions about everyday life:

  1. What kind of place do you prefer for studying, and why?
  2. Has your daily journey changed in recent years?
  3. What do you usually do when you need a short break?
Answer pattern: Answer the question first, then add a reason or example. “I usually study in a quiet room because interruptions break my concentration. Before an exam, I also leave my phone outside.”

Do not force every answer to be long. Aim for enough detail to sound natural without turning a simple question into a rehearsed speech.

Part 2: build a one-minute cue-card plan

Practice cue card: Describe a place where you learned something useful. Say where it was, what you learned, who helped you, and explain why you still remember it.

In your preparation minute, write short prompts rather than complete sentences: place → skill → person → one moment → impact. Then speak for up to two minutes, moving through those points as a story.

If you lose a word, paraphrase it. If you lose your place, return to the next prompt. Both are more useful than stopping to search for an impressive expression.

Part 3: explain and qualify an opinion

Part 3 questions are more abstract. Continue the learning theme:

  1. Should schools teach more practical skills?
  2. How has technology changed the way adults learn?
  3. Can people learn as effectively on their own as with a teacher?

Build an answer with a view, a reason, an example and a qualification. For example, independent learning offers flexibility, but feedback from a teacher can reveal errors a learner does not notice.

A repeatable 15-minute recording routine

  1. Record three Part 1 answers without writing a script.
  2. Give yourself one minute to plan the cue card, then record a long turn.
  3. Answer one Part 3 question with a reason and a counterpoint.
  4. Listen once for flow and once for accuracy. Note one change for the next attempt.

For fluency, notice long pauses and repeated fillers. For vocabulary, listen for vague words you could replace precisely. For grammar, check whether past experiences and future plans use consistent tense. For pronunciation, check whether a listener could follow your words without effort.
